I am very happy to write recommendation letters, and I most often write for
physics and computational science students. A good letter is specific: it
tells a story about what *you* did, how you think, and how you handle hard
things. I can only do that if you give me the raw material. The more you
give me, the stronger the letter I can write.

## Key points

-   Send **everything below** in **a single email**, in electronic form
    (pdf, Word, text file, or links). Please no paper copies.
-   Send it at least **2 weeks** before the deadline. For graduate school
    applications, **4 weeks** is better.
-   I will **only** write confidential letters.
-   If you want multiple letters, send the information for all of them at
    the same time so nothing gets buried in my inbox. (Med school letters
    go through Interfolio, so this doesn't apply there.)
-   If you haven't heard from me, it's fine to send a reminder one week
    before the deadline and again two days before.

## What I need

### 1. The basics

1.  Your full name, the name you go by, and the pronouns you use.
    [Why do I ask for your pronouns?](https://www.mypronouns.org/)
2.  **What you are applying for**: graduate school, REU or internship, a
    job, a scholarship or honor, medical/dental/vet school. This tells me
    the level and kind of detail to include.
3.  **Why you are asking me**, which courses or projects I know you from,
    and **who else is writing letters** for you. This helps my letter
    complement theirs.

### 2. Your projects (the most useful part)

Choose **two or three projects** that you are proud of. They can come from
a course, research, a capstone, an internship, a hackathon, or something
you did on your own. For each one, write a short paragraph (rough is
fine) that covers:

-   **The problem**: what were you trying to figure out or build?
-   **Your role**: what did *you* do, specifically? If it was a team
    project, what was your part?
-   **Methods and tools**: the physics, the code, the data, the
    equipment, the languages and libraries.
-   **What went wrong**: where did you get stuck, and how did you get
    unstuck? Stories about struggle and recovery are often the most
    convincing parts of a letter.
-   **The result**: what did you find or make, and what would you do
    next?
-   **A link**, if there is one: a GitHub repo, notebook, poster, talk,
    paper, or report.

If you took one of my courses, tell me which assignment or project you
would want me to remember, and why.

### 3. In your own words

Answer these in a few sentences each. Informal is fine; I'm mining them
for specifics, not editing your prose.

-   What are you proudest of from your time at MSU, and why?
-   What have you done that I might not have seen (helping classmates,
    learning something on your own, leading a group, going to office
    hours with a hard question)?
-   Why do you want this opportunity, and how does it connect to what you
    want to do next?
-   What should a reader know about you that your transcript and CV can't
    show?
-   Is there anything in your record I should address, such as a rough
    semester, a gap, or a change of direction? I'd rather frame it well
    than have a reader guess.

This is **not** the time for modesty. Remind me of how good you are so I
can say it.

### 4. Your documents

1.  A **bullet-point list of things you want me to talk about**. This
    isn't a draft for me to copy. It's a reminder of what I should
    include: prizes, research results, how we met, and anecdotes
    (for example, "first author as a junior" or "debugged the group's
    simulation code"). Specific beats general.
2.  Your **current transcript**. Unofficial is fine; MSU students can
    download a grade report from StuInfo.
3.  Your **resume and/or CV**.
4.  Your **personal statement(s)** or application essay. A rough draft is
    fine if the final isn't ready, but tell me it's a draft.
5.  Test scores (**GRE, MCAT**, etc.) if you have them and they are
    relevant.
6.  If you took one of my senior capstone courses: one or two papers you
    wrote for it.

### 5. Logistics

1.  A **list of programs or organizations** you're applying to, with due
    dates. (Not needed for med school letters.)
2.  The **name and title** of the person receiving the letter, if
    relevant.
3.  Any **forms** that need my signature, with your information
    **already filled in**.
4.  For electronic submissions: the **URLs or email addresses** where
    letters go. (Interfolio applicants can skip this; Interfolio will email
    me.)
5.  For letters that must be mailed: the addresses, plus **addressed,
    stamped envelopes**. Leave the return address blank and I'll fill it in.

## A few extra notes by application type

**Graduate school or REU.** Tell me which research areas and which
faculty you're interested in, and what computational or experimental
skills you bring (languages, data analysis, instrumentation, HPC). If you
have research experience, tell me what you contributed and what you
learned.

**Industry or internship.** Include the job description or a link, and
tell me which of your skills and projects you most want highlighted.

**Medical, dental, or vet school.** Do you have a specialization in mind
(oncology, pediatrics, large-animal medicine, reconstructive dentistry...)?
If so, what led you to it? Your project stories still matter: I like to
write about how you think, not only your grades.

**Scholarships and honors.** Send the selection criteria, and tell me
which of them you think you meet most strongly.

## What makes a helpful email

Here's a quick example of the difference between a weak and a strong
project description.

> **Weaker:** "I did a research project on simulating gravitational
> waves. It was a lot of work and I learned a lot."
>
> **Stronger:** "I wrote a Python code to model the inspiral of a binary
> black hole system using the post-Newtonian approximation. My first
> version drifted badly in energy, and I spent two weeks working out that
> my time step was too large near merger. I switched to an adaptive
> integrator and validated against published waveforms. Repo is linked
> below."

The second one gives me something I can actually write about.
